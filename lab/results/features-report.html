The dictionary inside GPT-2

A language model holds more concepts than it has dimensions, so it stores them layered on top of each other — and every direction in its own coordinate system comes out a blend. This is what happens when you train a sparse dictionary to pull the parts back out: 4,096 learned features, of which 32 fire per token, and they carry 98% of what the model was doing.

02 — The dictionary

What each feature answers to, and what it writes

Every feature has two halves, and they are measured from completely different places. What fires it comes from the corpus — the contexts where it activates most across 120,000 tokens of web text. What it writes comes from the weights alone — its decoder direction pushed through the model's own output readout. Nothing forces the two to agree. Where they do, the feature is doing one legible job.

04 — Superposition

The model's own coordinates really are blends

This is the claim the whole method rests on, and it can be measured directly. Take the same activations, and ask how selective each unit is: gather the tokens it fires hardest on and measure their entropy. A unit answering to one tight family of tokens scores low. A unit answering to everything scores high.

Read in the coordinate system the model happens to store its state in, almost nothing is selective. Read in the learned sparse basis, most things are.

05 — Depth

The same dictionary at three depths

Run it again at layers 2, 6 and 10 and the character of what it finds shifts. Early features are compact and token-local — easy to reconstruct sparsely, tightly bound to specific tokens, mostly completing words. Deeper features are harder to compress, answer to context rather than to any single token, and hit the output far harder when injected.

Selectivity entropy climbs from 0.45 at layer 2 to 0.64 at layer 10 while reconstruction gets harder — the deep features are not worse, they are less about which token is present. And they take over the output in 7 of 8 injections, against 3 of 8 early on: closer to the decision, a single feature carries more of it.

06 — Scale

A model six times bigger holds cleaner features — but the metric says otherwise

The same dictionary, run on gpt2-large (774M, 36 layers, 1280-dimensional). Sparsity, expansion ratio, tokens per feature and epochs are all held fixed, so only the model changes. Judged on selectivity, it looks worse: median entropy rises from 0.44 to 0.51. Judged on almost anything else, it is better — byte-fragment features disappear completely, and nearly every feature now keys on a real word.

The two readings are the same fact. What the bigger model stops producing is the single-token detector, down from a third of features to one in eight. What it produces instead are features that answer to several related words at once — and an entropy score measures how concentrated a feature is on one token, so it penalizes a feature precisely for being about a concept rather than a string.

What a concept feature looks like

Depth is the confound, not scale. Layer 18 of 36 is the same relative depth as gpt2's layer 6, but it has had three times as much processing behind it — and the depth sweep above already showed entropy climbing with depth inside a single model. Matching relative depth necessarily unmatches absolute depth, so the honest comparison is the middle column: gpt2-large at layer 6, where it is measurably cleaner.

The causal numbers move for the same reason. Injecting at layer 6 of gpt2-large leaves 30 layers of downstream processing to wash the signal out, against 6 in gpt2. That gap is what the drop in takeover measures — not feature quality.

07 — Method

How it was built, and what it does not show

An overcomplete autoencoder is trained on the layer's residual stream: it encodes each 768-dimensional state into 4,096 features, keeps only the 32 largest, and decodes back. It has to reconstruct the state through that bottleneck, so the features it learns are whatever the state is actually made of. Decoder columns are held to unit norm; features that stop firing are resampled onto the inputs the dictionary reconstructs worst. Roughly two minutes, on a laptop CPU, no API.

Two things that had to be found by measurement

Position 0 has to be excluded. GPT-2's first position is an attention sink: its residual norm at layer 6 is about 3,050 against 89 everywhere else. Left in, that single 34× outlier dominates a squared-error objective and hands most of the dictionary to one degenerate token.

The textbook sparsity penalty is dominated at this scale. The classic formulation buys sparsity with an L1 penalty on the code. Matched to the same sparsity on the same budget, it explains 38% of the variance against 77%, and leaves 31 features selective against 113. L1 shrinks every coefficient to get sparse, load-bearing ones included; keeping the k largest and penalizing none of them costs nothing on the survivors.

Honest scope

This runs on weights held locally, and it claims nothing about any hosted model's internals. The dictionary is small: 4,096 features over 120,000 tokens, where production ones are millions of features over billions. At this size many features are token-identity detectors rather than the abstract concepts larger dictionaries surface, and a handful lock onto byte fragments of the tokenizer rather than anything meaningful. The selectivity metric measures token selectivity only — as the scale section shows, that penalizes exactly the features that answer to a concept rather than a string, so it should not be read as an interpretability score across models of different sizes or depths. The scale comparison rests on 32 inspected features per run, one layer per model. What holds up: the fidelity number, the gap between features and basis directions, and the causal takeovers — each reproducible with one command.
